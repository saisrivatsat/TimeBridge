Knowledge base

Why time works this way.

No single person invented time. People gradually invented ways to observe, divide, standardize, and synchronize it.

01

What is time?

Time is not a clock. A clock counts a repeating process—such as a pendulum, quartz vibration, or atomic transition—and displays the accumulated intervals.

NIST explanation ↗
02

What is a time zone?

A time zone is a set of civil-time rules: its offset from UTC, its boundaries, and any seasonal changes. Governments choose these rules, so borders do not follow longitude perfectly.

IANA time-zone data ↗
03

What is daylight saving?

DST advances local clocks for part of the year. Rules differ by place and can change. In the U.S., participating areas currently move forward on the second Sunday in March and back on the first Sunday in November.

NIST local-time FAQ ↗
04

What is the reference?

UTC is today’s international reference time scale. Civil time zones are expressed as offsets from it. UTC is produced from atomic-clock data and kept close to Earth-rotation time.

A short history

From shadows to atomic time

  1. c. 3500 BCE

    Shadows divide the day

    Egyptian obelisks used moving shadows to separate morning and afternoon.

  2. c. 1500 BCE

    Portable sundials and water clocks

    Early devices made timekeeping useful away from a single monument and after sunset.

  3. 14th century

    Public mechanical clocks

    Large weight-driven clocks began appearing in European city towers.

  4. November 18, 1883

    North American railway time

    Railroads adopted standard regional times to replace dozens of conflicting local sun times.

  5. 1884

    Greenwich becomes the common meridian

    An international conference selected Greenwich as zero longitude and recommended a universal day.

  6. 1918

    U.S. federal standard time

    The Standard Time Act established federal oversight of time-zone boundaries.

  7. 1967

    The atomic second

    The SI second was defined using a transition of the cesium-133 atom instead of Earth’s variable rotation.

  8. Today

    UTC and the IANA database

    Atomic laboratories coordinate UTC while the IANA database records changing civil-time rules used by computers.

Decode the labels

ET is safer than always saying EST

“Standard” and “daylight” labels depend on the date. The generic family label avoids promising the wrong seasonal offset.

EasternETEST · UTC−5EDT · UTC−4
CentralCTCST · UTC−6CDT · UTC−5
MountainMTMST · UTC−7MDT · UTC−6
PacificPTPST · UTC−8PDT · UTC−7

Abbreviations are not globally unique—CST can refer to Central Standard Time or China Standard Time. TimeBridge Link calculates with region/city identifiers such as America/Chicago.
